# Sec 2 English — Exam Component Guide (Singapore MOE Syllabus)

---

## 1. SITUATIONAL WRITING (Email / Letter / Report / Article)

**What it actually tests:** can you write for a *specific purpose, audience, and format*, fast and accurately, using info from the given stimulus (usually a notice, flyer, or set of pictures/bullet points).

**Marking is split into two parts — know this, it changes how you write:**
- **Content marks**: did you cover ALL the required points from the question/stimulus? Missing even one required point = automatic loss of content marks, no matter how well-written the rest is.
- **Language marks**: register (tone — formal vs informal), grammar, format correctness.

**The #1 mistake:** students write a nice-sounding piece but miss 1–2 of the bullet points the question asked for. **Always underline every requirement in the question before you write**, then tick them off as you go. Content marks are the easiest marks in the whole paper if you're organised — don't throw them away.

**Format essentials:**
- **Email/Letter**: Subject line (if email), salutation matching register (Dear Sir/Madam for formal; Dear [name] for informal), clear paragraphing, appropriate sign-off (Yours faithfully vs Yours sincerely vs just your name for informal)
- **Report**: Needs a title, may need sub-headings, written in a more objective/factual tone, often third person
- **Article**: Needs a catchy headline, more engaging/narrative tone allowed, written for a general audience (e.g. school magazine)

**Register (formal vs informal) — how to tell which one to use:**
- Formal: writing to a stranger, an authority figure, a company, "Dear Sir/Madam," no contractions (don't → do not), no slang
- Informal: writing to a friend, contractions are fine, more casual tone — but NOT sloppy grammar, still needs correct English

**Exam technique:** Spend 2–3 minutes just planning — list out every required point from the question, decide the format, decide the register. This planning time pays for itself; rushing in without a plan is how points get missed.

## 2. CONTINUOUS WRITING (Compo / Narrative Essay)

**What it actually tests:** storytelling ability, language range, and the ability to sustain a coherent narrative — usually marked out of two components too: **Content & Organisation** and **Language**.

**Content & Organisation marks come from:**
- Relevance to the topic/picture prompt (does your story actually match what was given?)
- A clear story arc: **situation → complication → climax → resolution** — a story that just "happens" with no build-up or tension scores low even if the grammar is perfect
- Good pacing — don't spend 200 of your 350 words on the introduction and then rush the ending in 2 sentences

**Language marks come from:**
- Range of vocabulary (not just "big words" — *precise*, *varied* words)
- Sentence structure variety (mixing short punchy sentences with longer descriptive ones)
- Use of literary devices where natural: dialogue, sensory description, figurative language — but only if it fits, forced imagery reads worse than plain clear writing
- Grammatical accuracy — one strong essay full of errors will still be capped low

**The biggest score-killer:** writing a "template" story that doesn't actually answer the prompt. If the prompt says "Write about a time you had to make a difficult decision," your story MUST clearly show a decision and its difficulty — not just loosely relate to the theme.

**Planning technique (do this in your first 3–5 minutes):**
1. Pick a prompt you can build genuine tension/conflict around — not necessarily your most "interesting" idea, but the one you can structure clearly
2. Sketch the arc: opening hook → what's the complication? → what's the turning point/climax? → how does it resolve, with some reflection/insight at the end
3. Decide your opening line before you start writing continuous prose — a strong hook (not "One day, I...") sets the tone for the whole piece

**Common weak opening lines to avoid:** "It was a normal day and I was doing my normal routine..." — instead, drop the reader straight into a moment of action, feeling, or dialogue.

## 3. COMPREHENSION + SUMMARY

### Comprehension (short-answer questions)

**Key principle: answers must come from the text, in your own words where the question demands it, and must match the number of marks given.**

- If a question is worth 2 marks, you generally need 2 distinct points from the text
- **"In your own words" questions**: you CANNOT copy the phrase directly from the passage — you must paraphrase, or you lose the mark even if the content is right
- **Vocabulary-in-context questions**: don't just give a dictionary definition — explain what the word means *as used in this specific sentence/context*
- **Inference questions** ("What can you tell about X's feelings...?"): the answer isn't stated directly — you have to read between the lines using evidence from the text, then explain what that evidence suggests

**Common mistake:** copying long chunks of the passage instead of extracting just the relevant part, or lifting the exact words when the question specifically asks "in your own words."

### Summary Writing

**What it tests:** can you identify the key points in a passage and condense them into your own concise words, within a strict word limit.

- You will usually be given a specific *focus* (e.g. "summarise the problems faced by X" — not the whole passage, just that angle)
- **Content points = most of the marks.** Each valid point from the text = 1 mark, up to a cap. Style/language is a smaller portion.
- Must be in **your own words** — same idea as comprehension, lifting text directly loses marks
- Must stay within or close to the word limit — going significantly over usually means marks get capped or excess is disregarded

**Technique:**
1. Read the focus of the question carefully — this determines WHICH parts of the passage are even relevant; irrelevant points (even if true) don't earn marks and waste your word count
2. Underline/highlight every relevant point in the passage first
3. Rewrite each point in your own words — don't just remove a few words from the original sentence, actually rephrase the structure
4. Link points smoothly into flowing prose, not just a bullet list

## 4. EDITING + VISUAL TEXT

### Editing

**What it tests:** can you spot and correct grammar/spelling errors in a short passage — usually one error per line.

- Common error types to watch for: subject-verb agreement, wrong tense, wrong preposition, spelling, missing/wrong article (a/an/the), wrong word form (e.g. "success" vs "successful")
- **Technique:** read each line SLOWLY on its own — reading too fast, your brain auto-corrects errors without you noticing. Read it almost line by line, not as flowing paragraph.
- Write the correction clearly — if the marker can't tell what your correction actually is, no mark.

### Visual Text (comprehension of an ad/poster/infographic)

**What it tests:** can you extract information from a non-linear text format (layout, images, font size, colour, symbols all carry meaning, not just the words).

- Questions often ask you to identify: the purpose of the text, the target audience, and specific details (prices, dates, conditions)
- **Techniques used in visual texts to look out for**: bold/large font = emphasis, images/colours to attract attention, bullet points/callout boxes to highlight key selling points, symbols (ticks, stars) to signal features
- When asked "why did the writer use [X font/colour/image]?" — you must explain the EFFECT it has on the reader/target audience, not just describe what it looks like

## QUICK REFERENCE: What loses the most marks, by component

| Component | #1 mark-loser |
|---|---|
| Situational Writing | Missing a required content point |
| Continuous Writing | Story doesn't clearly address the prompt / weak structure |
| Comprehension | Copying text directly when "own words" is required |
| Summary | Including irrelevant points / going over word limit / lifting phrases |
| Editing | Reading too fast and missing errors |
| Visual Text | Describing the visual instead of explaining its effect on the reader |
