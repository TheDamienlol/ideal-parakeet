## 1. FACTORS, MULTIPLES, HCF & LCM

**Factor** of a number = divides into it exactly (no remainder). **Multiple** = the number times something.

**HCF (Highest Common Factor)** — the largest number that divides into BOTH numbers exactly.
**LCM (Lowest Common Multiple)** — the smallest number that BOTH numbers divide into exactly.

**Method (prime factorisation — most reliable for bigger numbers):**
1. Break each number into prime factors (use a factor tree)
2. HCF = multiply the **common** primes, taking the **lowest power** of each
3. LCM = multiply **all** primes appearing in either number, taking the **highest power** of each

**Worked example:** Find HCF and LCM of 36 and 48.
- 36 = 2² × 3²
- 48 = 2⁴ × 3
- HCF = 2² × 3¹ = 4 × 3 = **12** (lowest power of common primes: 2² not 2⁴, 3¹ not 3²)
- LCM = 2⁴ × 3² = 16 × 9 = **144** (highest power of every prime that appears)

**How to know which to use in a word problem (this is what actually trips people up):**
- **HCF** = when you're **splitting/grouping things into equal-sized groups** with nothing left over ("largest number of identical bags," "biggest square tiles to exactly cover a floor")
- **LCM** = when things are **happening repeatedly and you want to know when they coincide again** ("two bells ring every X and Y minutes, when do they ring together again," "buses leaving at different intervals")

## 2. LAWS OF INDICES

| Rule | Formula |
|---|---|
| Multiplying same base | aᵐ × aⁿ = a**ᵐ⁺ⁿ** |
| Dividing same base | aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = a**ᵐ⁻ⁿ** |
| Power of a power | (aᵐ)ⁿ = a**ᵐⁿ** |
| Zero index | a⁰ = **1** (any nonzero number to the power 0) |
| Negative index | a⁻ⁿ = **1/aⁿ** |
| Fractional index | a^(1/n) = **ⁿ√a** |
| Power of a product | (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ |

**Most forgotten rule:** a⁰ = 1. Students panic and think "0 to any power should relate to 0" — no, it's the *number* raised to the power **0**, and any nonzero number to the power 0 is always 1.

**Worked example:** Simplify (2x³)² ÷ x⁴
- (2x³)² = 4x⁶ (square BOTH the 2 and the x³ — common mistake: forgetting to square the coefficient)
- 4x⁶ ÷ x⁴ = 4x**²**

**Trap to watch:** negative indices don't make the number negative — a⁻² = 1/a², which is still positive if a is positive.

## 3. DIRECTED NUMBERS (Negatives) — the #1 silent mark-killer

**Multiplication/Division sign rules:**
- (+) × (+) = + | (−) × (−) = + | (+) × (−) = − | (−) × (+) = −
- Simple rule: **same signs → positive, different signs → negative**

**Addition/Subtraction — think of it as "adding a negative = going backward on the number line":**
- 5 + (−3) = 5 − 3 = 2
- 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8 (subtracting a negative = adding — this specific one gets missed constantly)

**Where this silently kills marks across the WHOLE paper, not just "directed numbers" questions:**
- Expanding brackets with a negative in front: −(x − 3) = −x **+ 3** (sign flips for BOTH terms, not just the first)
- Substituting negative values into formulas, e.g. if x = −2, then x² = 4 (positive!) but −x² = −4 (different from (−x)²)
- Solving equations with negative coefficients

**Fix:** whenever a negative sign is involved, physically slow down and rewrite the step rather than doing it mentally — this is genuinely the highest-ROI habit change for your whole exam, not just this topic.

## 4. EXPANSION & FACTORISATION

**Must-memorise identities:**
- (a + b)² = a² **+ 2ab** + b²
- (a − b)² = a² **− 2ab** + b²
- (a + b)(a − b) = a² **− b²** (difference of two squares — no middle term!)

**Most forgotten part:** the middle term (2ab) when squaring a binomial. Students often write (a+b)² = a² + b² — this is WRONG, missing the 2ab term entirely.

**Worked example:** Expand (2x − 3)²
- = (2x)² − 2(2x)(3) + 3²
- = 4x² − 12x + 9

**Factorisation is just expansion in reverse** — recognise the pattern:
- x² − 9 → this is a² − b² pattern (x² − 3²) → factorises to (x + 3)(x − 3)
- x² + 6x + 9 → this is a² + 2ab + b² pattern → factorises to (x + 3)²

**General factorisation (common factor / grouping):** always check for a common factor FIRST before trying anything else — e.g. 2x² + 4x = 2x(x + 2), don't skip straight to complex methods.

## 5. LINEAR EQUATIONS & INEQUALITIES

**Golden rule for equations:** whatever you do to one side, you MUST do to the other, to keep it balanced.

**Worked example:** Solve 3x − 5 = 2x + 7
- 3x − 2x = 7 + 5 (move x-terms to one side, numbers to the other — signs flip when you move terms across the "=")
- x = 12

**Inequalities work the same way, EXCEPT:**
> **When you multiply or divide both sides by a NEGATIVE number, you must flip the inequality sign.**

**Worked example:** Solve −2x > 8
- Divide both sides by −2, AND flip the sign: x **<** −4 (not x > −4 — this is the single most commonly forgotten rule in this topic)

**Quick sanity check trick:** test a number back into the original inequality to see if your answer direction makes sense.

## 6. ANGLE PROPERTIES

**Core facts:**
- Angles on a straight line = **180°**
- Angles at a point (full circle) = **360°**
- Angle sum of a triangle = **180°**
- Exterior angle of a triangle = sum of the two interior opposite angles
- Sum of exterior angles of ANY polygon = **360°** (always, regardless of number of sides)
- Sum of interior angles of a polygon = (n − 2) × 180°, where n = number of sides

**Parallel line angle rules — the most confused topic here:**

| Angle pair | Relationship |
|---|---|
| Corresponding angles | Equal (same position, "F-shape") |
| Alternate angles | Equal ("Z-shape") |
| Co-interior angles | Add up to 180° ("C-shape" / "U-shape") |

**Exam trick:** these rules ONLY apply when the lines are confirmed **parallel** (marked with arrows on the diagram, or stated in the question) — using them without parallel lines is a common error when students see a diagram that just "looks" like the pattern.

## 7. PERIMETER, AREA, VOLUME

**Key formulas:**
- Area of triangle = ½ × base × height
- Area of circle = πr² | Circumference = 2πr (careful: radius, not diameter — huge common mistake)
- Area of trapezium = ½ × (sum of parallel sides) × height
- Volume of prism = cross-sectional area × length
- Volume of cylinder = πr² × height

**Units trap (very commonly tested, very commonly lost):**
- Area units are always **squared** (cm², m²)
- Volume units are always **cubed** (cm³, m³)
- If converting units (e.g. cm to m for area), you must convert using the SQUARE of the conversion factor, not just the factor itself: 1m² = 100cm × 100cm = 10,000cm², NOT 100cm²

## 8. RATIO, RATE & PROPORTION

**Direct proportion:** as one quantity increases, the other increases at the same rate (y = kx). E.g. more workers → more work done in the same time.

**Inverse proportion:** as one quantity increases, the other decreases (y = k/x). E.g. more workers → LESS time needed to finish the same job.

**How to tell which applies in a word problem:** ask yourself — "if I double one quantity, does the other also roughly double (direct), or does it roughly halve (inverse)?" This mental check catches most setup errors before you even start calculating.

**Worked example (inverse):** 4 workers take 6 days to build a wall. How many days for 8 workers?
- More workers → fewer days (inverse)
- 4 × 6 = 8 × d → d = 24/8 = **3 days**

## 9. PERCENTAGE (incl. % Change)

**% change = (change ÷ ORIGINAL value) × 100**

**The #1 mistake:** using the NEW value as the denominator instead of the original. Always ask: "what was it BEFORE the change?" — that's your denominator, always.

**Worked example:** A price increases from $80 to $100. Find the % increase.
- Change = 100 − 80 = 20
- % increase = (20 ÷ **80**) × 100 = **25%** (NOT 20/100 — that's the wrong-denominator trap)

**Reverse percentage (also frequently forgotten):** if a price AFTER a 20% discount is $80, the original price is NOT found by adding 20% of $80 — you must recognise $80 = 80% of the original, so original = 80 ÷ 0.8 = $100.
